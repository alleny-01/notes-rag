# How memory becomes durable learning

Learning improves when new material is retrieved rather than only reread. Retrieval means attempting to recall an idea before looking back at the source. The effort of recalling gives a learner information about what is secure and what needs another pass.

Spacing matters because memory changes between study sessions. A short delay makes recall slightly harder, and that desirable difficulty strengthens the next retrieval. Repeating the same fact immediately can create familiarity without proving that it can be recalled later.

Feedback should be specific. When an answer is wrong, the most useful correction identifies the missing idea and reconnects it to the original explanation. A vague mark of right or wrong gives less guidance for the next attempt.

Interleaving is the practice of mixing related problem types. It helps a learner decide which method applies instead of relying on a predictable sequence. It can feel slower than blocked practice because the choice of method is part of the work.

Good notes preserve the reason behind a claim, not only its conclusion. A source passage, a concise explanation, and a question that tests the relationship between them make later revision more reliable. When a learner can point to the passage that supports an answer, uncertainty becomes something concrete to investigate.

For a revision session, begin with one focused question, attempt an answer from memory, then compare it with the source. Record the gap in a short sentence. That small loop turns a large set of notes into a sequence of manageable decisions.
